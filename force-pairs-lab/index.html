Force Pairs

Force Pairs

Newton's third law: when two objects interact, they exert forces on each other. The two forces are equal in size, opposite in direction, act on different objects, and are the same type of force (push and push, gravity and gravity).

The four screens

  • Push: two skaters push off each other. Change their masses, the push force, and who does the pushing. The force graph always shows mirror-image forces, while the lighter skater gets the bigger acceleration (a = F / m).
  • Collide: crash two vehicles in slow motion. The force-vs-time graph works like a pair of force sensors: the curves are equal and opposite for every mass, speed, and bounciness.
  • Launch: throttle a rocket off the pad. Three color-coded force pairs (rocket and gas, rocket and Earth, rocket and pad) show that partners act on different objects.
  • Real World: a scored challenge. Students identify the partner force in eight everyday scenes, get feedback aimed at the specific misconception behind each wrong answer, then see both arrows drawn on the scene.

For teachers

Learning goals. Students can (1) identify a third-law pair by naming the two objects and swapping them ("A pushes B" and "B pushes A"), (2) explain why the forces are equal even when masses or speeds differ, (3) use a = F / m to explain why equal forces cause different accelerations, and (4) tell a third-law pair apart from two balanced forces acting on one object.

Misconceptions targeted. The heavier or faster object pushes harder. The object that "starts it" pushes harder. Force pairs cancel, so nothing should move. Weight and the normal force are a third-law pair. Rockets push against the ground or the air.

Model notes. Skaters push with a steady force (smoothed at the start and end) until their arms have extended another 0.26 m; the ice is frictionless. Vehicle crumple zones are springs with a crush time of about 63 ms, and "bounciness" sets how much of the stored energy returns. The rocket's mass stays constant (no fuel burn) and there is no air resistance. g = 9.8 m/s².

Key ideas

Every force pair, from a swimmer's stroke to a rocket launch, follows the same four rules.

  • Equal in size

    The two forces always have the same size, even when one object is much heavier or moving much faster.

  • Opposite in direction

    If A pushes B to the right, then B pushes A to the left.

  • On two different objects

    One force acts on A and the other acts on B. Because they act on different objects, they never cancel each other out.

  • Same type of force

    A push pairs with a push, and gravity pairs with gravity. Both forces come from one interaction.

Find the partner: swap the objects

  • Blue pushes Red⇄Red pushes Blue
  • Rocket pushes gas down⇄Gas pushes rocket up
  • Foot pushes ground back⇄Ground pushes foot forward
  • Earth pulls book down⇄Book pulls Earth up

a = F / m  The same size force gives a small mass a big acceleration and a big mass a small one. That is why the lighter skater speeds away, and why Earth doesn't move noticeably when you jump.

Common mix-ups

  • The heavier or faster object pushes harder.Both forces are equal. Mass changes how much each object accelerates, not the size of the force.
  • Whoever starts the push pushes harder.It doesn't matter who starts. Both forces appear at the same instant and are the same size.
  • Force pairs cancel, so nothing can move.The two forces act on different objects. To predict one object's motion, add up only the forces acting on that object.
  • Weight and the table's push are a pair.Both act on the same object (the book) and they are different types of force. The partner of the book's weight is the book's pull on Earth.
  • Rockets push against the air or the ground.A rocket pushes on its own exhaust gas, and the gas pushes back on the rocket. That works even in empty space.
